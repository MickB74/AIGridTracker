Kentucky — Andy Barr (Republican)

New senator Mixed

Result: won. Called. 55.0%–42.9%. Takes the seat held by Mitch McConnell (Republican). — source, read 2026-11-21.

At stake in this state: 40 data centers · 0.6 TWh/year · 29 local moratoriums in force

U.S. Representative for Kentucky's 6th District since 2013.

The record in short: Barr campaigned on powering AI data centers with Kentucky coal, introduced a 2025 bill to give large AI data centers and the power plants serving them priority federal permitting, and says he personally lobbied the Energy Secretary for the $100 billion Paducah data center campus. He also voted for the House-passed Ratepayer Protection Act, said in the October debate that Kentuckians should not pay higher utility bills because of a data center, and says localities should decide whether to host them.

What they have done — bills, votes, letters, formal acts

  • Voted Yea on H.R. 9340, the Ratepayer Protection Act, which passed the House 417-3 under suspension of the rules. (2026-09-16 — Clerk of the U.S. House, Roll Call 312)
  • Quoted in the Department of Energy's announcement of the Paducah Site data center campus (a more than $100 billion private investment), calling it historic and saying he personally advocated with the Energy Secretary to make the project happen. (2026-07-29 — U.S. Department of Energy press release)
  • Introduced H.R. 5927, the Securing Reliable Power for Advanced Technologies Act (sponsor; 0 cosponsors listed; referred to House Financial Services). The bill would amend the Defense Production Act to let the President designate 'critical artificial intelligence infrastructure' projects as priority national defense projects with concurrent federal permitting on a consolidated schedule. Its definition covers AI data centers with at least 50 megawatts of connected load, dispatchable baseload generation of at least 100 megawatts that does not rely primarily on intermittent renewables and is committed to serve them, associated transmission, and fuel supply infrastructure including coal mines and gas facilities. The portion of the text read contains no ratepayer cost-allocation provision; the full text was not read to the end. (2025-11-07 — Congress.gov)

What they have said — promises, not actions

  • In the only televised Senate debate (WKYT), said Kentuckians should not pay higher utility bills if a data center moves into their town (reporter's paraphrase), that the U.S. must win the AI race against China, and that he believes in local control. (2026-10-19 — Yahoo News (syndicated; byline McKenna Horsley, original outlet not shown on the page))
  • Told Spectrum News 1, in a campaign profile, that the country needs more data centers and more affordable, reliable power, and that localities should decide whether to host them. The article also says that in February he supported the administration's loosening of emission standards for coal-fired power plants while advocating for more hyper-scale data centers (reporter's characterisation). (2026-10-14 — Spectrum News 1 Kentucky)
  • Campaign statement to WPSD, responding to Booker's attacks over the Paducah project, saying local communities rather than Washington should decide whether data centers are built. (2026-09-30 — WPSD Local 6)
  • Senate campaign release on his coal plan says that as electricity demand from AI and data centers grows, reliable around-the-clock power is a national security issue, and lists among legislation he has 'led and supported' measures to speed permitting for power plants and transmission and to 'support AI data centers'. The release names no bill numbers. (2026-01-05 — Andy Barr for Senate press release)
  • Campaign energy plan (PDF) lists 'Support AI data centers and advanced manufacturing that depend on affordable, nonstop power' among its legislative aims, alongside speeding permits for power plants and transmission lines, and says the plan will lower energy costs for Kentuckians. (date not recorded — Andy Barr for Senate, 'Plan to Revive Coal Jobs and Power the Technologies of the Future')

How this page is built

Who won is read from the results file, never inferred. Each result carries the page it was read from, the date, and whether it is a call or a state certification.

Every winner was researched again after the election. Their own releases, congress.gov, state legislative and executive records, and the reputable press were searched for bills, votes, vetoes, letters, hearing questions and on-the-record statements about data centers, the power bills and water they affect, and the tax breaks and siting rules that govern them. Each item kept was opened at its source and confirmed to name the winner doing or saying the thing. An item whose page could not be opened was left out rather than published from a search snippet.

Silence is disclosed, never scored. “Lean” summarises only the cited items below it, on one axis: does this candidate's documented position make data centers pay their own way and give the host community a say? It is not an overall rating, not a grade, and not an endorsement. Candidates with no located record are left unscored.

This is not the officials scorecard. The A–F scorecard grades sitting officials on a documented action. A winner who already holds office may carry a grade here; a newcomer cannot, because a campaign promise is not an action.

Where the other 82 candidates went. Until the election this address tracked all 117 filed candidates. The people who lost hold no federal office and their campaign statements bind nobody, so the page now covers the winners only. Sitting senators who lost keep their record on the senators page until their term ends.

Lean labels

  • Guardrails — Documented support for making data centers carry their own costs, for community say over siting, or for a pause.
  • Mixed — Documented on both sides — courts the buildout but backs some ratepayer or community protection.
  • Accelerate — Documented emphasis on building faster — competitiveness, incentives, or siting help — without a matching cost or community safeguard on the record.
  • Consensus vote only — On the record only for a near-unanimous vote (the House passed the Ratepayer Protection Act 417-3). Shown, not scored — a vote nearly everyone cast says little about any one member.
  • No record found — No documented statement or action on data centers located. Not scored — the gap is disclosed, not punished.
