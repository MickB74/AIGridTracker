Five Governors-Elect Promised a Data Center Pause. Here Is What a Governor Can Actually Do.

Nov 17, 2026 · GridWatch AI
election 2026 governors Texas Georgia Ohio Kansas Wisconsin Michigan Arizona moratorium utility commission

Of 34 winning candidates for governor, 32 backed tighter rules on data centers and 12 backed a ban or moratorium, according to a tally cited by the Associated Press. The pledges are real. So are the limits: a governor cannot repeal a tax break alone, cannot freeze a permit already issued, and an executive-order pause may not survive a lawsuit. A state-by-state read of what was promised and which lever it depends on.

Of the 34 candidates who won a governor's race this month, 32 supported stronger regulation of data centers and 12 supported a ban or moratorium. That count comes from the research group Data Center Watch and was reported by the Associated Press on Nov. 6.

If you live near a proposed site, a governor-elect's moratorium pledge sounds like the end of your fight. It is not, and the reason matters for what you do at the next county meeting. A governor has three real levers: executive orders over state agencies, appointments to the commissions that regulate utilities and permits, and a signature on whatever the legislature passes. Almost every pledge below depends on one of those, and each has a limit.

Texas: a pause tied to the Legislature

Gina Hinojosa defeated Gov. Greg Abbott and takes office Jan. 19. During the campaign she pledged a moratorium on data center approvals that would last until the Legislature meets and passes protections, the Texas Tribune reported. Her stated priorities are union hiring, water protection, no nondisclosure agreements, no shifting of electricity costs onto other customers, and an end to the sales-tax subsidy.

Two limits apply. First, the tax break. A 2025 state Comptroller report put the data center sales-tax exemption at more than $1 billion that year, rising to about $1.75 billion a year by 2030, and a Rice University economist told CBS Austin that the governor cannot repeal it on her own. That takes the Legislature, where Democrats won the House 78-63 but Republicans kept the Senate 19-12, according to a post-election client alert from the law firm Brownstein. The same alert notes that repeal has support on both sides, since Abbott had proposed it too.

Second, existing projects. A University of Houston lecturer told ABC13 Houston that a pause applies to future projects and that already-permitted ones cannot be frozen. What a Texas governor can do over time is reshape the Public Utility Commission and the state environmental agency through appointments.

For a Texas resident, that means a project with permits in hand is still your county's problem, and the place to watch is the session that opens in January.

Georgia: an executive order of uncertain footing

Keisha Lance Bottoms, who won the Georgia governor's race, said she would sign an executive order imposing a temporary moratorium on new data center approvals. Local station WRDW reported the pause could last a month to six months or longer and would mainly affect future rezonings and tax deals, not approved projects.

The Atlanta Journal-Constitution reported that it is unclear whether such an order would survive a legal challenge, and that the industry will not support a statewide moratorium. In Georgia, zoning belongs to counties and cities. An order aimed at state agencies does not rewrite a county's zoning code, which is why the local moratoriums already on our tracker still matter.

Georgia voters also flipped the Public Service Commission to a 3-2 Democratic majority. One Democratic commissioner, Peter Hubbard, who was re-elected, said stopping data centers is not in his power or his interest, Atlanta News First reported. What a commission controls is who pays for the power plants and lines, which is the part that reaches your bill.

Ohio, Kansas, Wisconsin

  • Ohio. Amy Acton backs a conditional moratorium: projects would have to cover all their utility costs, use union labor and locate on old industrial sites. She also promised to claw back incentives, according to Scripps News.
  • Kansas. AP called the race for Cindy Holscher, NPR reported on Nov. 12. She called for a moratorium on new data centers during the campaign.
  • Wisconsin. David Crowley moved to backing a moratorium during the campaign, per the AP report above. We found no local reporting on how he intends to carry it out.

None of these three has published a mechanism or a date. Until one does, treat each as a stated intention.

The quieter lever: who sits on the utility commission

Five incoming governors campaigned on appointing utility commissioners who would put ratepayers first: Acton in Ohio, Jocelyn Benson in Michigan, Crowley in Wisconsin, Aaron Ford in Nevada and Hannah Pingree in Maine, according to Heatmap News. Ford also pledged to pause and review data center tax abatements, per a Columbia University energy policy analysis.

This is slower than a moratorium and likely to last longer. A commission decides whether a utility's new substation for a data center is paid for by the data center or spread across every customer. Appointments take effect as terms expire, so the change arrives over years.

Two results to keep straight:

  • Arizona. Democrats Jonathon Hill and Clara Pratte won seats on the Corporation Commission, where Republicans had held all five, KJZZ reported. By our arithmetic that leaves a 3-2 Republican majority. The commission did not change hands.
  • Michigan. Voters passed a measure barring regulated utilities from making campaign contributions with 81% of the vote, and the state's two largest utilities have sued to block it, Latitude Media reported. Advocates expect Benson to pursue a pause, but her transition team did not comment, and the same report notes a pause may need the Legislature because siting there is largely local.

How much of the buildout this touches

The Columbia analysis estimates that the five states where the governorship changed party (Texas, Georgia, Ohio, Iowa and Nevada) hold about 142.5 gigawatts of announced data center demand, roughly 41% of the national pipeline. Its authors estimate slowdowns could affect up to about 50 gigawatts over two to three years, or under 10 gigawatts if the pauses are short.

Those are estimates of announced projects, many of which would never have been built. They show where the pressure is, not what will happen.

What to do with this

  1. Do not stand down locally. No pledge above stops a project that already has its permits, and none takes effect before January.
  2. Ask which lever. When an official cites the governor-elect's moratorium, ask whether it is an executive order, a bill or an appointment, and when it takes effect.
  3. Watch the commission. Rate cases and large-load tariffs are where the cost question is decided. Your state's commission and its open dockets are on our PUC directory.
  4. Keep your own moratorium current. A local pause with an end date still expires on that date, whoever is governor. Check yours on the moratorium tracker.
