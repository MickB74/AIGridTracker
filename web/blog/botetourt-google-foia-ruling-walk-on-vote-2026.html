A Judge Stopped Google's Virginia Data Center Over a Vote the County Never Described. The Project Is Not Dead.

Nov 26, 2026 · GridWatch AI
Virginia Botetourt County Google open meetings FOIA lawsuits performance agreement accountability

A Virginia circuit judge voided Botetourt County's 2025 deal with Google and ordered construction stopped, because supervisors voted on it after a closed session without saying what it was. The county can hold a proper hearing and vote again. Here is what the ruling says, what it does not, and what to check in your own county's minutes.

On Monday, November 23, a circuit judge in Botetourt County, Virginia ordered construction to stop on Google's data center campus at the Botetourt Center at Greenfield. He did not rule that the project is bad for the county, uses too much water, or violates zoning. He ruled that the Board of Supervisors never told the public what it was voting on.

That is a narrower win than the headlines suggest, and a more useful one. Most residents fighting a data center cannot prove the project is harmful. Many can check whether their local board followed its own state's open-meetings law.

What the judge ruled

Substitute Judge Michael Doucette found that the board violated the Virginia Freedom of Information Act at its June 24, 2025 meeting, when it approved a performance agreement with Helio Capital LLC, the Google affiliate behind the project. According to Cardinal News, which reported from the written ruling, the order does three things:

  • It rescinds every action the county has taken on the project since that meeting.
  • It bars further action under the agreement. Virginia Business reports the injunction forbids Helio Capital, and anyone acting on its behalf, from doing construction on the campus.
  • It tells the board how to fix the problem: hold a public hearing that "conforms to" the state's open-meetings law, then vote.

The judge took the request for attorney fees under advisement and declined to order the county to follow the law in future, writing that if it does not, "the result will be the same as today."

What happened at the 2025 meeting

The facts were not really in dispute. Cardinal News reports that the county's lawyer did not contest them and argued instead that the county had done enough.

As the two outlets describe the record: the board went into closed session on a motion that cited economic development in the Amsterdam District, where the industrial park sits. The motion did not mention a performance agreement or a data center. The closed session ran well over an hour. When the board came back into public session, the chair called up "walk-on item number one," asked whether anyone had questions about the performance agreement resolution, and the board voted 5-0 with no discussion. The item had not been on the published agenda.

The public announcement that the buyer was Google came later the same day, after a recess, at about 5:03 p.m. The county argued that this later announcement satisfied the law. The judge called that a "tortured reading of the statute." Virginia Business quotes him from the bench: "I have a fundamental disagreement about whether you can disclose 3.5 hours after the vote."

The county's lawyer described the problems as "technical violations" and said "magic words" were not used. The judge's answer, as quoted by Cardinal News, is the part worth remembering: the act "is a technical and strictly procedural act," so "'magic words' must be used and 'technical violations' are not excused."

The statute he applied

The rule is one sentence in the Code of Virginia. Section 2.2-3711(B) says that nothing "adopted, passed or agreed to in a closed meeting shall become effective" unless the public body reconvenes in open meeting and votes on a resolution "that shall have its substance reasonably identified in the open meeting."

The judge found that "walk-on item number one" did not identify anything, and concluded the board's action "never became effective." Cardinal News reports he relied on a 2023 Virginia Supreme Court decision that voided Fairfax County's rewritten zoning ordinance over an open-meetings violation.

Two neighboring sections matter to anyone thinking about a similar case in Virginia. Section 2.2-3712(A) requires the motion to enter closed session to identify the subject matter, state the purpose, and cite the exemption, and says a general reference is not enough. Section 2.2-3713 lets any person denied rights under the act petition for an injunction, says a single violation is enough to invoke the remedy, calls for a hearing within seven days, and entitles a petitioner who substantially prevails to costs and attorney fees unless special circumstances make that unjust.

The speed is real. Robert Dallas, a retired anesthesiologist who lives about half a mile from the site, filed on November 2. The judge refused to dismiss the case on November 10 and ruled on November 23. That is three weeks from filing to an order halting a project Virginia Business values at $3 billion.

What the ruling does not do

It does not kill the project. Every source that covered the ruling says the same thing: the county can hold a compliant public hearing and vote again, and the result may be identical. It can also appeal. The county's statement said it was "evaluating its options," and Google told WSLS it was evaluating the ruling.

It does not make the site go quiet overnight. Board chair Mac Scothorn said Tuesday that construction activity would be minimal through the holidays and limited to keeping the site in compliance with its permits. The Roanoke Rambler reports a county spokesman said wind-down activity could continue for several days, possibly a week.

It does not settle the money. Google paid about $14.06 million for the 312 acres and pledged $4 million for county projects. Testimony showed $2.5 million of that pledge has already been transferred and spent on things like ambulances and fire trucks. Lawyers for the county and Helio argued that this, and grading that began in September, made the suit too late. The judge was not persuaded, but nobody has said what happens to money already spent under an agreement a court now says never took effect. The Rambler reports the board pulled two agenda items on Tuesday that depended on Google funds.

It does not bind other courts. This is one circuit judge's ruling. Megan Rhyne of the Virginia Coalition for Open Government told Virginia Business it is uncommon for a judge to invalidate a public body's action over a FOIA violation, and that it is too early to know the wider effect.

And the board that votes next is not the board that voted in 2025. Two of the five supervisors from that meeting are gone, replaced by members elected a few months after the deal was announced.

What to check in your own county

This ruling applies Virginia law. Every state has its own open-meetings act, with its own notice rules, deadlines and remedies, and a violation that voids a vote in Virginia may not void one where you live. So the lesson is not "sue over the agenda." It is that the paper trail of the approval meeting is public, checkable, and sometimes decisive.

For the meeting where your project's key agreement was approved, pull the published agenda, the minutes, and the video if one exists, and ask:

  1. Was the item on the agenda that was published before the meeting, or added during it?
  2. If there was a closed session, what did the motion to close say? Did it name the subject, or only a category like "economic development"?
  3. When the board came back and voted, did anyone say out loud what the resolution was and what it committed the county to?
  4. Was the public told at the time of the vote, or later?
  5. What does your state's law say happens to a vote that skipped those steps, and how long do you have to challenge it?

Question five needs a lawyer licensed in your state. The first four need a records request and an evening. In Botetourt, residents had been saying for more than a year that the process was wrong. What changed the outcome was finding the specific sentence in the statute that the meeting did not satisfy.
