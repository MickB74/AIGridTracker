Week in Review: San Francisco Bans Data Centers in the Birthplace of AI, Texas's Own Utility Regulator Sues to Keep Data Center Locations Secret, and Memphis's Moratorium Vote Ends in a Brawl

Oct 11, 2026 · GridWatch AI
week in review moratorium California Texas Tennessee North Carolina Georgia Virginia water transparency community

This week San Francisco and Oakland each passed 45-day data center moratoriums in the city where OpenAI, Anthropic, and most of the AI industry is headquartered, and Texas's own Public Utility Commission sued Attorney General Ken Paxton's office to keep data center location records secret. Memphis's council postponed its moratorium vote twice amid a meeting that ended in a physical altercation, Raleigh passed a six-month pause after activists asked for 32 months, Georgia and Virginia regulators cited data centers for environmental and permit violations, and a federal judge upheld Nashville's authority to pause a project near the city zoo. Plus: Denver7 counted nearly 500 U.S. communities that have now paused or banned data centers.

Welcome back to the GridWatch AI Week in Review — our Sunday roundup of the most important data center stories from the past seven days, what they mean for communities, and what you can learn from each one.

1. San Francisco and Oakland each pass data center moratoriums — in the city where the AI industry itself is headquartered

What happened: The San Francisco Board of Supervisors passed a 45-day moratorium on new data centers, which Bloomberg described as happening "in the hometown of AI," while CBS News reported Oakland's City Council approved its own 45-day pause the same week. The Oaklandside reported Oakland's vote came amid broader local backlash to AI data center growth, and other Bay Area cities were reported to be reviewing similar policies.

Why it matters: San Francisco is where OpenAI, Anthropic, and a large share of the companies driving data center demand are physically headquartered. A 45-day pause doesn't block anything permanently, but the symbolism is hard to miss: the industry's hometown joining the roughly 500 communities nationwide (see story 7) that have decided they need a timeout before the next project gets approved.

What to learn — a short pause is a tool, not a verdict: 45 days is barely enough time to hold one or two hearings, which means the real fight over whether San Francisco and Oakland get a longer moratorium, a permanent ordinance, or nothing at all is still ahead. If your own council passes a short pause, treat it as a deadline to organize before, not a result to celebrate. See the full moratorium tracker and the California state page.

Sources: Bloomberg, Oct 7; CBS News, Oct 7; The Oaklandside, Oct 7

2. Texas's own Public Utility Commission sues the state's attorney general to keep data center locations secret

What happened: The Texas Public Utility Commission sued Attorney General Ken Paxton's office to block the release of data center location records, according to the Texas Tribune, KXAN Austin and Texas Scorecard. The outlets' headlines describe a dispute over whether the records have to be disclosed; none of them report who originally requested the locations or when a ruling is expected.

Why it matters: We covered Paxton's office opening an investigation into hundreds of Texas data centers that never answered a mandatory water-use survey in last month's roundup. This week a different arm of Texas state government — the utility regulator that is supposed to oversee grid reliability — went to court specifically to keep facility locations out of public view. Two state agencies pulling in opposite directions on data center transparency in the same month is worth watching regardless of how the suit resolves.

What to learn — "confidential" is a legal category, not a given, and it's worth contesting: Utilities and regulators often claim location or load data is protected as a trade secret or critical-infrastructure information. That claim isn't automatically correct — many states have a public-interest override or require the agency to justify the withholding specifically, not categorically. If a utility or state agency in your area refuses to disclose a data center's location or power draw, ask specifically which statute they're citing and whether it's been tested in court. See the Texas state page and our PUC directory for how to file your own request.

Sources: Texas Tribune, Oct 6; KXAN Austin, Oct 9; Texas Scorecard, Oct 8

3. Memphis's data center moratorium vote is postponed twice — the second time after a meeting ends in a physical altercation

What happened: The Memphis City Council's vote on a data center moratorium ordinance, originally scheduled for Tuesday, was postponed twice within the same week, according to localmemphis.com and FOX13 Memphis. After the second postponement, Tri-State Defender reported "fists fly" among attendees and The Commercial Appeal and Fox News both described the meeting devolving into chaos before it ended. Separately, localmemphis.com reported Shelby County's own commission introduced a parallel moratorium ordinance covering the unincorporated county.

Why it matters: A procedural postponement is routine; two postponements in a week, one ending in a physical confrontation, is a sign the underlying disagreement in Memphis — over an xAI-linked data center buildout that has also drawn a wrongful-death suit and a noise lawsuit we covered in September's roundup — has outpaced the council's ability to manage the room. A city and a county both drafting their own ordinances at the same time also means residents need to track two separate votes, not one.

What to learn — know which body actually has jurisdiction over the parcel you care about: A data center sited within city limits is governed by the city council's zoning code; one in unincorporated county land answers to the county commission instead, and the two bodies can move on completely different timelines, as Memphis and Shelby County are doing right now. Before you track a vote, confirm with your clerk's office which jurisdiction the specific parcel sits in. See Memphis's community page and the Tennessee state page.

Sources: localmemphis.com, Oct 6; FOX13 Memphis, Oct 7; Tri-State Defender, Oct 7; The Commercial Appeal, Oct 7

4. Raleigh passes a six-month data center moratorium — activists had asked for 32 months

What happened: The Raleigh City Council passed a six-month moratorium on new data center approvals, according to WRAL and the Raleigh News & Observer. The Business Journals reported that opposition groups had specifically asked the council for a 32-month pause, and the city's own notice, published by the City of Raleigh, confirms the adopted pause runs six months from enactment.

Why it matters: This is the gap between "a moratorium passed" and "the moratorium residents wanted" made concrete with real numbers: six months versus the 32 months activists requested, a more than five-to-one difference. A headline that says a city "passed a moratorium" doesn't tell you which side of that gap your own council landed on.

What to learn — ask for the proposed duration in writing before the vote, not after: When your council is considering a pause, get the specific number of months on the record ahead of the hearing — from the ordinance text, not a summary — and compare it to what residents are asking for. Raleigh's six months buys time for one planning cycle; a group asking for 32 months is asking for enough time to rewrite the zoning code entirely. Those are different asks and your testimony should match which one your council is actually voting on. See our model CBA clause library for the kind of time-bound language negotiators use, and the North Carolina state page.

Sources: WRAL, Oct 6; Raleigh News & Observer, Oct 7; The Business Journals, Oct 7; City of Raleigh, Oct 8

5. Georgia fines two companies over "pop-up" power plant permits, and Virginia regulators cite a Google data center for sediment violations

What happened: Georgia regulators fined two companies a combined $41,500 over permit violations tied to "pop-up" power plants built to supply data centers, according to WSB-TV and the Atlanta Journal-Constitution. Separately in Virginia, state regulators cited a Google data center project in Chesterfield County for what WRIC ABC 8News reported as seven environmental violations tied to sediment runoff, after Richmond Times-Dispatch first reported Google was cited for the runoff days earlier.

Why it matters: Both are enforcement catching up after the fact, the same pattern we flagged with New Jersey's record fine against a Vineland data center last month: a facility operates out of compliance for a period before a state agency issues a citation or fine. A $41,500 fine against a project representing hundreds of millions in capital spending is not, on its own, much of a deterrent — whether it changes behavior depends on whether the violation gets fixed, not just fined.

What to learn — a facility's environmental record is split across more than one agency, and each keeps its own log: An air or power-generation permit violation (Georgia's case) and a stormwater/sediment-control violation (Virginia's case) are enforced by different divisions, often under different statutes, even at the same company. If you want the complete compliance picture for a project near you, you generally have to request records from each relevant division separately — the zoning office's file won't show you either one. See the Georgia and Virginia state pages.

Sources: WSB-TV, Oct 6; Atlanta Journal-Constitution, Oct 8; WRIC ABC 8News, Oct 9; Richmond Times-Dispatch, Oct 6

6. A federal judge dismisses a developer's lawsuit over Nashville's handling of a data center near the zoo

What happened: A federal judge dismissed a lawsuit DC Blox had filed challenging Metro Nashville's actions on a proposed data center project near the Nashville Zoo, according to WSMV and Axios. The Center Square reported the company said it plans to amend its complaint and continue pursuing the case rather than drop it.

Why it matters: A developer suing over a moratorium or a permit denial is one of the more common pressure tactics communities face once they pause or reject a project, and this is a data point in the other direction: a court sided with the city's authority to act, at least on this round of claims. It's not the end of the case — DC Blox is reportedly amending its complaint — but it is evidence that a moratorium ordinance, drafted carefully, can survive a legal challenge.

What to learn — "the developer will just sue" is a real risk, but not an automatic loss: Communities often hear that passing a moratorium or denying a permit invites an unwinnable lawsuit. Nashville's result (so far) and the dismissal of a similar developer challenge to North Carolina's Chatham County ban we covered in September both suggest a well-drafted ordinance, grounded in the locality's actual zoning authority, has a real chance of holding up. See Nashville's community page.

Sources: WSMV, Oct 7; Axios, Oct 8; The Center Square, Oct 8

7. Nearly 500 communities nationwide have now paused or banned data centers

Beyond the stories above, Denver7 reported this week that Denver's own advisory-group move on data centers makes it "part of a growing trend" it counted at nearly 500 U.S. communities that have paused or banned data center development. A sample of what moved in just the past seven days:

Locality State Action Duration / scope
Lexington-Fayette KY Moratorium extended Until October 2027
Manatee County FL Moratorium approved 12 months
Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes OK Tribal ban enacted 3 years
Easthampton MA Moratorium approved 18 months
Brownsville TX Moratorium, first vote Under consideration
Niles Township IN Moratorium approved Under consideration

What to learn — extensions are their own kind of signal: Lexington didn't just pass a pause, it extended one it already had, out to a specific date two years away — a sign the council still doesn't consider its original review finished. If your own community already has a moratorium on the books, the date it's set to expire (or whether it gets extended before that date) tells you exactly when the real decision will actually get made. See the full moratorium tracker for all tracked jurisdictions and their expiration dates.

Sources: Denver7, Oct 7; Lexington Herald Leader, Oct 7 and WKYT, Oct 8 (Lexington-Fayette); WUSF, Oct 9 and Florida Politics, Oct 8 (Manatee County); News 9, Oct 4 and KOSU, Oct 5 (Cheyenne and Arapaho Tribes); Daily Hampshire Gazette, Oct 8 (Easthampton)
