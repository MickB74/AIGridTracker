Week in Review: Oregon's Governor Pauses Data Center Permits Statewide, Milwaukee Bans New Ones for a Year, and a $3 Billion Massachusetts Project Dies on a Technicality

Oct 18, 2026 · GridWatch AI
week in review moratorium Oregon Wisconsin Texas Tennessee Massachusetts New Jersey West Virginia water community

Oregon Gov. Tina Kotek ordered state regulators to stop issuing air and water permits for new data centers until mid-2027, and Milwaukee's Common Council unanimously passed its own one-year moratorium. In Texas, Hood County commissioners rejected a developer's settlement offer and permit appeal despite an explicit threat of a lawsuit, while in Massachusetts, a Planning Board quietly killed a $3-4 billion data center campus by refusing to extend a permit the developer let lapse. Plus: Memphis and Shelby County both advance competing moratorium ordinances, a month-old New Jersey diesel spill still hasn't drawn a fine, and West Virginia's governor strikes a deal to relocate a rare 200-tree chestnut grove instead of clearing it.

Welcome back to the GridWatch AI Week in Review — our Sunday roundup of the most important data center stories from the past seven days, what they mean for communities, and what you can learn from each one.

1. Oregon's governor orders a statewide pause on data center environmental permits — but says she can't go further than that

What happened: Gov. Tina Kotek directed the Oregon Department of Environmental Quality to stop processing and issuing air and water permits for new or expanded data centers until July 1, 2027, according to OPB, KPTV and the Oregon Capital Chronicle. The pause, issued in an Oct. 9 letter to DEQ Director Leah Feldon, also covers municipalities seeking to modify wastewater permits to handle data center discharge. Kotek's office told reporters that Oregon law does not give her the authority to impose a true statewide moratorium on private land without the Legislature acting — her pause only reaches the permits her own executive branch issues.

Why it matters: This is the broadest state-level permitting freeze we've covered, and it followed a narrower September order that paused data center requests on state-owned land only. Oregon Rural Action said the new pause could affect a proposed Amazon hyperscale facility in Boardman; Amazon called the move disappointing and said it could cost the state jobs and investment. Both candidates in Oregon's governor's race, Kotek and Republican Christine Drazan, say they support some form of moratorium — meaning the fight here isn't really over whether to act, but over how far an executive can act without the Legislature.

What to learn — an executive order is only as strong as the authority behind it: Kotek's own office is the one saying she can't ban data centers on private land by herself. If your governor or mayor issues an order, find out in the same news cycle which specific permits or approvals it actually freezes, because "pause" can mean anything from a total halt to a narrow hold on one agency's paperwork. Oregon's Data Center Advisory Committee is taking public comment on its preliminary recommendations through Oct. 24 — a concrete, date-stamped way for residents in any state to see what a formal rulemaking process looks like. See the moratorium tracker and the Oregon state page.

Sources: OPB, Oct 13; KPTV, Oct 13; Oregon Capital Chronicle, Oct 13

2. Milwaukee's Common Council unanimously passes a one-year data center moratorium

What happened: The Milwaukee Common Council voted unanimously to adopt a one-year moratorium on new data center construction and expansion, according to Urban Milwaukee and Hoodline. The pause runs for one year or until the city finishes new zoning rules defining data centers, whichever comes first, and state law would let the city extend it up to six more months "if absolutely necessary." Developers can still seek a site-specific planned-development rezoning, which requires its own public hearings.

Why it matters: Urban Milwaukee traces the push to a fight over a proposed 19,000-square-foot data center inside a redevelopment of a vacant Midtown Center Walmart; after sustained public opposition, the computing component was dropped from that project entirely before the citywide moratorium even passed. That is the sequence worth noting — organized opposition to one specific proposal is what produced a citywide policy, not the other way around.

What to learn — a single fight, won locally, is how citywide rules get written: Milwaukee's moratorium didn't start as a citywide campaign; it started as neighbors objecting to one redevelopment plan. If you're facing a single proposed site, documenting your specific objections on the record is also laying groundwork your city council can point to later if it decides to write a general ordinance. See the Wisconsin community page for Milwaukee and the moratorium tracker.

Sources: Urban Milwaukee, Oct 13; Hoodline, Oct 13

3. Hood County, Texas commissioners reject a developer's settlement offer — and its permit appeal — despite an explicit threat of a lawsuit

What happened: Hood County commissioners voted 3-1 to reject a settlement proposal from Comanche Circle developer Sailfish Investors, then voted unanimously to deny the company's appeal of its own permit applications, according to KERA News and The Cool Down. The rejected settlement would have shrunk the project's footprint from roughly 2,523 acres to about 1,375 acres in exchange for commitments on setbacks, cooling methods, light pollution and road repairs. In an Oct. 1 email to county officials first reported by KERA, Sailfish founder Ryan Hughes warned that rejecting the deal could lead to a lawsuit and a larger project built with fewer restrictions. After the vote, Hughes said he was ready for "the fight that would ensue."

Why it matters: This is a county commission turning down a developer's own stated alternative to litigation, in writing, immediately before the vote. Hood County has already been sued twice by data center developers this year, and two earlier attempts by its commissioners to pass a moratorium failed after a state lawmaker warned they were acting outside their authority — in Texas, counties generally lack the zoning power cities have. Commissioners here are navigating a case where saying no is a real legal risk, not a bluff, and they still said no.

What to learn — a developer's threat to sue over a smaller version of their own project is a negotiating position, not a fact about the law: A threat framed as "we'll build something bigger and worse if you don't take this deal" is still just a prediction about the next move, not a legal consequence of this one. If a developer tells your council or commission that rejecting a compromise invites something worse, ask your own counsel what authority the body actually has — the answer in Texas is that it depends heavily on whether you're a city or a county. See the Texas community page for Hood County and last month's roundup on developer lawsuits.

Sources: KERA News, Oct 13; The Cool Down, Oct 14

4. Memphis and Shelby County advance two separate, competing moratorium ordinances in the same week

What happened: The Memphis City Council voted 7-5 to postpone its data center moratorium ordinance to Oct. 20, its third delay since the chaotic meeting we covered last week, according to Action News 5. Separately, the Shelby County Commission — a different body covering only unincorporated county land — passed its own, narrower 12-month moratorium on first reading in a 5-4 vote with four commissioners abstaining, according to the Memphis Flyer and FOX13 Memphis. County Attorney Greg Barnes confirmed the county's version would only reach unincorporated areas, not the city. Opponents on the commission questioned why a pause was needed when, as County Director Ragsdale noted, no data center permits are currently pending in the county.

Why it matters: Two governing bodies, two different ordinances, two different timelines, covering two different pieces of the same county — this is the jurisdictional split we flagged as a teaching point last week, playing out in real time. A resident following "the Memphis data center fight" actually has to follow both votes separately to know whether a given parcel is covered.

What to learn — an ordinance that passes "first reading" has two more votes to go: Tennessee county ordinances require three separate readings before taking effect, and the Shelby County measure's 5-4 margin with four abstentions means it could still fail on a later reading. Don't report a multi-reading ordinance as "passed" until it clears its final vote — see the Shelby County ordinance's next scheduled stop, Oct. 21, and Memphis's community page.

Sources: Action News 5, Oct 13; Memphis Flyer, Oct 12; FOX13 Memphis, Oct 12

5. A Massachusetts Planning Board kills a $3-4 billion data center project by simply declining to extend its permit

What happened: The Westfield, Massachusetts Planning Board voted unanimously not to extend developer Servistar Realties' special permit for a 150-plus-acre data center campus, effectively ending a project the company had pursued since 2019, according to the Boston Globe and Connecticut Public. Board members cited a lack of progress since the permits were first approved in 2021: Servistar never purchased the land, let its LLC status expire in late 2024, and never recorded its special permit with the Hampden County Registry of Deeds. The developer's representative blamed delays on state data center tax incentives that Gov. Maura Healey froze applications for over the summer. Servistar can appeal within 20 days.

Why it matters: No council vote banned this project — a board simply declined to give an inactive permit more time, and a $3-4 billion campus died on paperwork. Westfield also has a citywide one-year data center moratorium in effect; City Councilor Kristen Mello noted a developer is free to try again once it expires next July, but said any new proposal would need stronger water-supply protections than this one had.

What to learn — track your local permit's actual expiration date, not just whether a project was "approved": An approval from years ago can quietly lapse if a developer never acts on it — and a planning board that won't extend it is functionally the same as a denial, without ever holding a vote that looks like one. If a project near you was approved long ago and you haven't heard updates, check whether its permit has an expiration clause and when it falls due. See our hidden-water-cost explainer for the kind of water-supply language Westfield's councilor says the next version will need.

Sources: Boston Globe, Oct 14; Connecticut Public, Oct 14

6. New Jersey's largest data center fuel spill still hasn't drawn a fine, a month later

What happened: Equinix told W.Media it is still finalizing a full incident report on the roughly 5,000-to-5,500-gallon diesel spill at its NY2 facility in Secaucus, first reported in September. Equinix now attributes the spill to a mechanical failure of two valves during routine fuel filtration, after Secaucus's mayor initially described it as a "computer glitch." New Jersey DEP said the diesel reached a small tributary of Anderson Creek but stayed contained short of the Hackensack River; crews recovered about 3,500 of the estimated gallons released. As of the most recent reporting, the state had not issued any fine or enforcement action against the company.

Why it matters: Rep. Nellie Pou called it the largest data center-related fuel spill on record in New Jersey and gave NJDEP an Oct. 9 deadline to respond with a full accounting. A month after the spill and roughly a week past that deadline, the public record still consists of the company's own evolving explanation and no stated penalty — the same enforcement-catches-up-later pattern we flagged with Vineland's fine and with Georgia and Virginia's citations last week.

What to learn — "diesel backup generator" means there's a fuel tank, and fuel tanks leak: Every data center of any size keeps diesel on hand for backup power, which means every data center of any size is a potential spill site, not just an electricity user. If you're negotiating with a developer, ask specifically about tank capacity, containment design, and who the facility is required to notify — and how fast — if a tank fails. See our CBA clause library for model language on incident reporting and the New Jersey state page.

Sources: W.Media, Oct 12

7. Also this week: a chestnut grove gets a reprieve, and three more moratoriums move

Beyond the stories above, West Virginia Gov. Patrick Morrisey announced a deal to relocate — rather than clear — a roughly 200-tree grove of blight-surviving American chestnuts at the former Clements State Tree Nursery in Mason County, with developer Nscale funding the move, according to Cybernews and Gadget Review. The grove sat on land slated for an Anthropic-linked data center campus; arborists are expected to relocate the trees during the 2027 dormant season. A sample of three more actions still in motion:

Locality State Action Status
Charlotte NC Moratorium extension Council vote set for Oct. 26; current 150-day pause expires Nov. 5
Minneapolis MN Moratorium nearing expiration Runs through Nov. 21; permanent zoning due the same month
Dover DE First-in-state pause advances City committee sent an 18-month pause to the full council

What to learn — "relocation" is a negotiated outcome, not a default protection: Nothing in federal or West Virginia law required Nscale to move these trees rather than clear them; the commitment came from public pressure plus a governor's announcement, the same kind of negotiated mitigation residents elsewhere have won on noise limits or water caps. If a site near you has a specific natural feature worth protecting, naming it explicitly in testimony is what gives a developer or official something concrete to agree to. See the moratorium tracker for every jurisdiction's status and deadline.

Sources: Cybernews, Oct 11; Gadget Review, Oct 11
