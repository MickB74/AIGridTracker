Week in Review: Data Centers Lose Nearly Every Ballot Measure Nationwide, Georgia Flips Its Utility Board, and OpenAI Launches a Post-Election Charm Offensive

Nov 8, 2026 · GridWatch AI
week in review moratorium election Ohio Georgia Texas Virginia New Jersey Maine Connecticut Idaho PUC community money

In Tuesday's elections, voters decided data-center ballot measures in roughly ten states and 41 local contests — the industry won one. Ohio's wave of local bans swept through Election Day and cost Vantage Data Centers a project in Oregon, Ohio; Georgia voters flipped control of the state's Public Service Commission; and Texas elected a governor who says she'll keep the state's data center moratorium going. OpenAI responded with a new policy framework promising more local input and a pledge to cover its own power costs. Plus: Bernie Sanders wants a national moratorium, and fresh bans in Maine, Connecticut and New Jersey.

Welcome back to the GridWatch AI Week in Review — our Sunday roundup of the most important data center stories from the past seven days, what they mean for communities, and what you can learn from each one. This week's roundup covers Tuesday's elections, which touched nearly every story we have.

1. Voters rejected data centers almost everywhere they were on the ballot

What happened: On Nov. 3, voters in roughly ten states decided data-center-related ballot measures spanning 41 local contests. The data-center industry won exactly one of them, according to CNBC and the Latin Times, which both published that tally after results came in. Mother Jones counted close to 40 towns that rejected data center projects outright, and Ballotpedia had flagged the roughly ten-state slate of measures before the vote. We first wrote about data centers becoming a cross-partisan political liability back in August, in Data Center Opposition Just Became a National Political Force — this week is that prediction arriving all at once.

Why it matters: A single-digit industry win rate across 41 separate local votes is not noise; it is the clearest national signal yet that opposing a data center is no longer a fringe local-politics position. Every council member and state legislator up in the next cycle saw these numbers too.

What to learn — a ballot-measure win is a mandate, not an ordinance: Voters approving a ban or a moratorium question tells elected officials what residents want, but in most places it still has to be implemented or re-passed as a specific ordinance with its own definitions, exemptions and enforcement mechanism — the same gap we flagged in Pittsburg, California two weeks ago, where a disputed definitions section nearly let a project through a pause voters thought was airtight. After a ballot win near you, the next question is which board writes the implementing ordinance, and when. See the moratorium tracker for how local actions turn into enforceable rules.

Sources: CNBC, Nov 4; Latin Times, Nov 6; Mother Jones, Nov 5; Ballotpedia

2. Ohio's moratorium wave swept through Election Day — and cost Vantage a project

What happened: Local data center bans "passed easily" across Ohio on Nov. 3, according to the Columbus Dispatch, and WYSO Public Radio reported voters across southwest Ohio pushed back against both data centers and the politicians who support them. Specific results piled up fast: Pataskala and the village of Granville both passed bans, per the Newark Advocate (the Licking County, Ohio paper — not to be confused with Newark, New Jersey, which had its own data center news this week, below); Urbana voters recalled their mayor and approved a ban in the same election, according to WDTN; and Upper Sandusky approved a ban citing farmland and water concerns, per Brownfield Ag News. In Oregon, Ohio, a Toledo suburb, voters approved a charter amendment restricting data centers — known locally as Issue 25 — and WTVG reports Vantage Data Centers has said it will not move forward with its project there as a result.

Why it matters: We counted 60 local Ohio moratorium actions in our September look at the state's wave; this week's results show the wave didn't crest, it kept building straight through a statewide election. The Vantage withdrawal is also a rarer thing than most of this week's headlines: a named operator confirming, on the record, that a specific local vote actually stopped a specific project — not just a pause while rules get written.

What to learn — a citizen-passed charter amendment is harder to undo than a council ordinance: Oregon, Ohio's restriction took the form of a charter amendment, which (unlike an ordinance a council adopted on its own vote) generally can't be repealed or weakened by that same council without going back to voters. If your community is choosing how to enact a restriction, ask whether a ballot-approved charter change is on the table — it changes who gets to undo it later. See the Ohio state page and the moratorium tracker.

Sources: Columbus Dispatch, Nov 6; WYSO, Nov 5; Newark Advocate, Nov 4; WDTN, Nov 4; Brownfield Ag News, Nov 6; WTVG/13abc, Nov 5

3. Georgia flips its utility board, and OpenAI answers there first

What happened: Georgia voters handed Democrats control of the state's Public Service Commission, according to The Daily Caller and WSB-TV — the same PSC listed in our own PUC directory as the body that regulates Georgia Power. WSB-TV reports the newly elected commissioner has said they support a data center moratorium. Separately this week, OpenAI said it would begin testing a "Grid Protectors" program that cuts its data centers' electricity draw during peak demand, starting with up to 1 GW of capacity in Georgia, according to Tom's Hardware and FourWeekMBA.

Why it matters: The Public Service Commission — not the local zoning board — is who approves Georgia Power's rate cases and decides whether ordinary ratepayers help cover the cost of serving a new data center campus. Flipping its majority changes the referee in that fight just as OpenAI is making Georgia the test case for its own voluntary demand-reduction pitch. We've covered Georgia data center fights before in our September look at Statesboro and Effingham County; this is the state-level piece of the same story.

What to learn — your PUC/PSC is often the most powerful official you've never heard of: A zoning board can deny a site plan; a PUC decides whether the rest of a utility's customers subsidize the power line built for one. Before a hearing, find out who regulates your state's utility — our PUC directory lists all 51 — and ask what rate case, if any, is open for the project near you. See the Georgia state page.

Sources: The Daily Caller, Nov 6; WSB-TV, Nov 5; Tom's Hardware, Nov 3; FourWeekMBA, Nov 2

4. Texas elects a governor who promises to keep the moratorium going — and the spin starts immediately

What happened: Gina Hinojosa, elected Texas's next governor, said she will keep the state's data center moratorium in place and pursue further legislation, according to Data Center Dynamics and KHOU.

Why it matters: Texas carries one of the largest pipelines of planned data center capacity in the country, so a governor committed to an active moratorium is a bigger lever than almost any single local vote this week. Within days, at least one financial newsletter was already arguing the market shouldn't worry much, framing Hinojosa as meaningfully different from national moratorium advocates like Sen. Bernie Sanders (more on him below) — a reminder that a campaign promise gets reinterpreted by interested parties the moment the polls close.

What to learn — track the bill, not the soundbite: "The governor supports a moratorium" and "the legislature passed a moratorium extension" are different facts, and only one of them is binding. Once a legislative session opens, look for the actual bill number and its committee status rather than re-reading the campaign-trail quote. See our Texas ERCOT queue explainer for how the state's existing freeze actually works on the ground, and the Texas state page.

Sources: Data Center Dynamics, Nov 5; KHOU, Nov 7

5. OpenAI's post-election pivot: more local input, and a pledge to cover power costs

What happened: OpenAI laid out a new policy framework for its data centers this week, according to Politico, which The New York Times reports includes giving local communities more say in siting decisions. Yahoo Finance reports the company also pledged to "pay the full cost of the electricity we use," so that households near its facilities aren't billed for its draw on the grid.

Why it matters: The complaint behind nearly every moratorium fight on this page — that ordinary ratepayers end up subsidizing a hyperscaler's grid hookup — is exactly what OpenAI is now promising, in public, to not do. That is a notable public commitment from a frontier AI lab, and also exactly the kind of pledge this site exists to track rather than take at face value.

What to learn — a company pledge is not a filed rate-case stipulation: We've been here before. Our look at Microsoft's ratepayer pledge found a gap between a company's public promise and what was actually on file with regulators. Before citing OpenAI's pledge at a hearing, ask your utility or PUC whether it appears as an actual rider or stipulation in an open rate case — not just a company blog post. See the PUC directory.

Sources: Politico, Nov 5; The New York Times, Nov 5; Yahoo Finance, Nov 6

6. Virginia: "the midterms' other big loser"

What happened: Politico called data centers the midterms' other big loser in Virginia, and WJLA reports Virginia voters backed candidates who ran on stricter data center regulation.

Why it matters: Virginia hosts the largest concentration of existing data centers in the country, so a political shift there carries more weight than a similar shift almost anywhere else. We've been tracking Virginia's decision calendar closely — see our September rundown of Loudoun's and Fairfax's votes and Loudoun's pause vote — and this week's results mean the next round of those votes happens under different political pressure than the last.

What to learn — "backed stricter regulation" can mean several different things: A candidate's data center position usually bundles several separate levers — siting authority, by-right approval repeal, ratepayer cost allocation, water permitting — that don't all move together. Ask a newly elected official which specific lever they intend to pull first. See the Virginia state page.

Sources: Politico, Nov 4; WJLA, Nov 5

7. Also this week: Sanders calls for a national moratorium, and more local bans in Maine, Connecticut and New Jersey

Sen. Bernie Sanders and other top Democrats called for a "national data center moratorium now" after this week's results, according to Quartz and CNBC. A federal moratorium has no realistic path through Congress this session, but it signals where part of the party wants to take this fight next.

Four more results to know about:

Locality State Action Status

What to learn — New Jersey's ban count is a trap, so don't quote a number you can't source yourself: When we tried to pin down New Jersey's statewide ban total in August, four different groups published four different totals — see Four Groups Counted New Jersey's Data Center Bans. They Got 37, 38, 50 and 69 — because they were counting different things. Treat any single "definitive" count of this week's results the same way: ask what, exactly, is being counted before you repeat it.
