434 House Races Have a Winner. Only 78 of Them Ever Said Anything Specific About Data Centers.

Nov 19, 2026 · GridWatch AI
election 2026 House New Jersey Maine Wyoming New York Virginia super PAC ratepayers accountability

Our House tracker now records a result for every one of the 440 seats on the ballot: 434 winners and six races still in a recount or runoff. Seventy-eight winners have a documented position on data centers. Another 240 are on the record only for one near-unanimous vote. The rest have said nothing we could find, including the new member for the South Jersey district where Vineland sits. What the results show, where they cut against the easy story, and how to look up your own district.

Every seat on the 2026 House ballot now has a result on our House race tracker: 434 with a winner and six still open. Each result links to the page it was read from. Most are news organizations' calls. Fifty are marked certified because the state itself labels its results official.

The New York Times counted 257 Democratic seats with seven races uncalled as of Nov. 17. That is the national picture. This post is about a narrower question that matters more if a data center is proposed near you: what has your representative actually said about it?

For most districts, the honest answer is very little.

The count

Of the 434 winners in our records:

  • 78 have a documented position on data centers. Of those, 59 lean toward what we call guardrails (data centers pay their own costs, or the community gets a say), 14 are mixed, and 5 lean toward building faster without a matching safeguard on the record.
  • 240 are on the record only for one vote. In September the House passed the Ratepayer Protection Act 417-3. A yes on a bill nearly everyone voted for tells you little about any one member, so we show it and do not score it.
  • 116 have no record we could find. Many of these are new members.

So roughly one winner in five has said something specific enough to hold them to.

Leaning toward faster buildout did not cost House members their seats

In the Senate, the two candidates whose records leaned toward faster buildout both lost. It would be tidy to report a pattern. The House data does not support one.

Of the 8 House candidates with that lean whose races are decided, 5 won. All five are incumbents. Three lost, including Mike Lawler in New York's 17th District, who had opposed his governor's data center moratorium, and Rob Wittman in Virginia's 1st.

The results cut the other way too. In Maine's 2nd District, Paul LePage, who described himself as a big fan of data centers (his campaign said afterward that he supports "sensible regulation," so we record him as mixed), won the open seat over a Democrat who favored ratepayer and community benefit benchmarks. And two Republicans with guardrails records lost their seats anyway: Gabe Evans in Colorado's 8th, the lead Republican sponsor of the Ratepayer Protection Act, and Jeff Van Drew in New Jersey's 2nd.

In House races, incumbency and the national mood decided more than any candidate's data center position did. Anyone telling you the House results were a referendum on data centers is ahead of the evidence.

New Jersey's 2nd: a new member with no record

This one is close to home for us. New Jersey's 2nd District covers the southern end of the state, including Vineland, where the state fined a data center $1.07 million in September.

Van Drew, the Republican incumbent, had introduced, with Rep. Riley, the FAIR Data Act, a bipartisan bill to stop data center projects from passing grid upgrade costs to residential customers. He lost to Democrat Zack Mullock.

We have found no documented statement or action by Mullock on data centers. That is not a criticism of him. It is a gap, and it may be a gap in our research. But it means the district with one of the most contested data centers in the state is now represented by someone whose position residents cannot look up. If you have a source, send it to us. If you live there, it is a fair first question to put to his office.

New members who did take a position

Several first-time winners arrive with something on the record:

  • Wyoming at-large: Chuck Gray, a Republican, said at an August forum that he opposes AI data centers, and Cowboy State Daily reported he alone among the candidates indicated a desire to ban them federally. The same article notes he voted in 2021 to keep Wyoming's data center tax exemption.
  • Utah's 1st: Ben McAdams, a Democrat, opposed the Stratos project in Box Elder County.
  • New Hampshire's 1st: Stefany Shaheen supported a moratorium, saying municipalities were not yet prepared, per Spectrum News.
  • Colorado's 1st: Melat Kiros supports a nationwide moratorium on data center construction, Drop Site News reported.
  • Connecticut's 1st: Luke Bronin has called for developers to pay their fair share, according to the American Prospect. The article does not quote him.

Opposition to data centers is not confined to one party. Gray and Kiros agree on little else.

How the AI industry's money did

Two networks of AI-focused super PACs spent in House general elections. Fortune tallied the results the day after the election, and we checked each race against our own records.

Candidates the networks backed who lost: Aaron Flint in Montana's 1st ($590,428), Laurie Buckhout in North Carolina's 1st ($509,067), Scott Singer in Florida's 25th ($857,426), Carlos De La Cruz in Texas's 35th ($1,492,321), Rhett Marques in Alabama's 2nd ($974,908) and Alex Mealer in Texas's 9th ($647,075).

Candidates they backed who won: Jared Moskowitz in Florida's 25th, Brian Poindexter in Ohio's 7th and Bob Brooks in Pennsylvania's 7th, all Democrats. Jace Yarbrough in Texas's 32nd was uncalled when Fortune published and has since been called the winner.

The dollar figures are Fortune's. One of the networks, Leading the Future, told TechCrunch before the election that it sees primaries as a better use of its money, and it plans to keep spending in 2028. That matters for the next cycle. A primary is where a candidate with no record acquires one, and where far fewer voters are watching.

Still open

Six races have no winner in our records:

  • Minnesota's 1st, North Carolina's 5th and 10th: recounts. In the first two the margin is a few hundred votes.
  • California's 23rd: not yet called.
  • Louisiana's 5th and 6th: runoffs on Dec. 12.

We will update each when it is settled. California counties certify by Dec. 3 and the state by Dec. 11, according to AP.

Look up your district

  1. Open the House tracker and type your state or your representative's name.
  2. If your representative has a cited record, read the items and the date we read them. A position from last spring may have moved.
  3. If the record is only the 417-3 vote, that is a starting point for a letter, not an answer. The question to ask is what they will do beyond a bill that nearly everyone supported.
  4. If there is no record, ask for one. A new member's staff is building its list of constituent concerns right now. Being on it early is worth more than being on it late.

The federal government does not decide whether a data center gets built in your county. Your county does. But Congress sets the rules for who pays for the transmission lines, and that is the part that shows up on your bill.
