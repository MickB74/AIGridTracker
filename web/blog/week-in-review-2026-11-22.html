Week in Review: Loudoun County Pauses Data Centers for a Year, Minneapolis's New Rules Die in a Failed Veto Override, and a New York Town's Ban Hits a Legal Snag

Nov 22, 2026 · GridWatch AI
week in review moratorium Virginia Minnesota California New York Pennsylvania Georgia Oregon community veto CBA

The country's largest data center market, Loudoun County, Virginia, paused new approvals for 12 months this week — and half a dozen other local governments landed in just as many different places on the same spectrum. Minneapolis's new data center ordinance died when the council fell short of the votes to override a mayoral veto; Rochester, Minnesota banned data centers outright with a 5-1 vote; San Francisco extended its own moratorium for the second time; a New York town's 3-2 vote to ban data centers may not survive a landowner protest petition; and Olyphant, Pennsylvania took a $101 million settlement instead of a fight. Plus: Effingham County, Georgia approved OpenAI's site plan while punting its moratorium decision to January, and Hillsboro, Oregon renewed its pause for a third time.

Welcome back to the GridWatch AI Week in Review — our Sunday roundup of the most important data center stories from the past seven days, what they mean for communities, and what you can learn from each one. This week's roundup runs from the country's single largest concentration of data centers to a town of about 11,000 people outside Scranton, and it shows the full range of ways a local government can respond to a project it didn't ask for.

1. Loudoun County, Virginia — the data center capital of the world pauses for a year

What happened: The Loudoun County Board of Supervisors voted to pause new data center and electrical substation applications for up to 12 months — the maximum a Virginia locality is allowed — in a vote on Nov. 17, according to Data Center Dynamics and FOX 5 DC. The county's own announcement reports the vote at 7-1-1, with Supervisor Kristen Umstattd of Leesburg opposed and Supervisor Caleb Kershner of Catoctin abstaining. Virginia Mercury and Commercial Observer both noted the pause is retroactive to Sept. 15, and that the county has 33 active applications already in its pipeline that the pause does not touch.

Why it matters: Loudoun County is "Data Center Alley" — the single largest concentration of data centers on the planet, and the place every other county's debate eventually gets compared to. A year-long pause there is a different kind of signal than the same pause in a town most people have never heard of.

What to learn — a "pause" and a "moratorium" aren't always the same legal tool, and the difference can decide what a county is allowed to do: Virginia Mercury reported that the Loudoun County Attorney's Office had already determined the Board does not have the legal authority to halt by-right administrative applications — only the newer category of applications that require a legislative vote. That is why county officials are careful to call this a "pause" on legislative applications rather than a blanket moratorium: it is the broadest tool state law actually gives them, not the broadest tool they might want. Before you cite a neighboring county's moratorium as a model, find out whether your own state's enabling law lets your locality do the same thing, or only part of it. See the Virginia state page and the moratorium tracker.

Sources: Data Center Dynamics, Nov 17; FOX 5 DC, Nov 17; Virginia Mercury, Nov 20; Commercial Observer, Nov 18

2. Minnesota, two cities, two outcomes: Minneapolis's rules die in a failed veto override, Rochester bans first

What happened: Minneapolis Mayor Jacob Frey vetoed the City Council's new data center ordinance on Nov. 19, and the council's attempt to override that veto failed 7-5 — two votes short of the nine required — at a special meeting on Nov. 20, according to MPR News, Axios Twin Cities and FOX 9 Minneapolis-St. Paul. The council had passed the ordinance 7-6 the week before; Frey objected to a last-minute amendment requiring case-by-case council approval of every new data center, calling it a "de facto ban." With the city's temporary moratorium also expiring that weekend, MPR News reported Minneapolis now has no data center regulations on size, noise, energy or water use at all, while the Planning Commission starts over with a public hearing set for Dec. 14 and a council vote for Dec. 17. A hundred miles south, KTTC reported the Rochester City Council passed its own one-year moratorium on a 5-1 vote on Nov. 17, even though city staff told the council there are no pending data center applications to pause.

Why it matters: We covered Minneapolis's push for standing rules instead of an outright ban two weeks ago in our last Week in Review — this week is what happened after the council actually tried to pass them. The ordinance is dead, not paused: Minneapolis will spend the next month with zero data center rules in place while a new draft works through the planning process.

What to learn — know the override number before the vote, not after: A council that passes something on a narrow 7-6 margin has, by definition, not secured the votes to survive a mayoral veto if most charters require a two-thirds majority to override one — Minneapolis needed nine of thirteen. If your city's data center ordinance clears committee by a one- or two-vote margin, that margin tells you whether a veto is survivable before the mayor ever uses one. Rochester took the simpler, blunter path — a pause needs only a majority, which is why it passed with no project even on file yet. See the Minnesota state page and the moratorium tracker.

Sources: MPR News, Nov 19 & Nov 20; Axios Twin Cities, Nov 19; FOX 9 Minneapolis-St. Paul, Nov 20; KTTC, Nov 16 & Nov 17

3. San Francisco extends its data center moratorium again — because its own report found gaps

What happened: The San Francisco Board of Supervisors voted on Nov. 17 to extend the city's data center moratorium roughly another 10 and a half months, to around September 2027, according to SFist and the San Francisco Standard. Both outlets reported the vote was unanimous except for Supervisor Connie Chan, who was absent. The Planning Department's own report, cited by both outlets, found data centers in the city are concentrated in southeast San Francisco, a part of the city that already carries a disproportionate share of its air pollution burden — though the report stopped short of finding that existing facilities are causing significant environmental harm on their own. Supervisor Shamann Walton, who sponsored the original pause in October, also sponsored this extension.

Why it matters: San Francisco's first 45-day pause is already on our moratorium tracker, where it's flagged as "fixed_undated" because early coverage couldn't agree on an effective date. This extension is the city buying itself more time precisely because its own study wasn't finished — not because of outside pressure. One specific project, the NOVVA data center in Bayview, had been planning to grow from 9 to 36 megawatts by 2028; that expansion stays frozen for another ten months.

What to learn — an unfinished environmental study is itself a reason to extend a pause, and that's by design: Several of the moratoriums we track exist specifically to buy time for a study the locality hasn't finished yet. When a report comes back incomplete or inconclusive, as San Francisco's did, that isn't a reason to let the pause lapse — in a well-run process it's the reason to extend it until the study is actually done. If your town's moratorium was passed "to study impacts," ask what specifically the study is supposed to answer, and whether an incomplete answer extends the pause or ends it. See the California state page and the community page for San Francisco.

Sources: SFist, Nov 17; San Francisco Standard, Nov 17; Mission Local, Nov 17

4. Lansing, New York votes to ban data centers 3-2 — but a landowner petition could void it

What happened: The Lansing Town Board voted 3-2 on Nov. 18 for a zoning change that would prohibit data centers and cryptocurrency mining facilities townwide, according to Fingerlakes1 and the Cornell Daily Sun. But a group of landowners — organized around TeraWulf, the company behind a proposed 300-megawatt AI data center on Cayuga Lake — delivered a written protest petition the day before the vote. Under New York Town Law Section 265, a valid protest signed by owners of a large enough share of the affected land raises the threshold for approval from a simple majority to three-fourths of the board — four of Lansing's five members, not three. Fingerlakes1 reported the town was still verifying whether the petition's signers actually own enough of the affected land to trigger that higher bar.

Why it matters: A 3-2 vote reads as a win in a headline. Whether it legally is one depends on a procedural question — acreage ownership percentages on a petition — that most residents watching a town board meeting have never heard of and that may not be resolved for days or weeks after the vote itself.

What to learn — in New York, and in some other states, a landowner protest petition can quietly change the number of votes a zoning change needs: This is the single most consequential thing to check after a close local vote on a data center ban or ordinance: does your state's zoning enabling law let a qualifying group of affected landowners — not voters generally, specifically landowners — force a supermajority? If it does, find out the exact threshold (acreage percentage, not signature count) before declaring the vote final. See the New York state page and the moratorium tracker for how we flag a status that hasn't actually settled yet.

Sources: Fingerlakes1, Nov 18 & Nov 20; Cornell Daily Sun, Nov 19 & Nov 21; WBNG, Nov 18

5. Olyphant, Pennsylvania takes the money instead of the fight: a $101 million settlement, no ban

What happened: Olyphant Borough Council voted 5-2 on Nov. 17 to settle its lawsuit with ERCOR Triboro LLC and approve a community benefits agreement worth $101 million over 15 years, clearing the way for the seven-building, roughly 470-acre Project Triboro data center campus, according to WVIA. Under the deal, the developer is allowed to skip the borough's conditional-use review process — the public hearing track the project would otherwise have had to clear — in exchange for guaranteed payments: $5 million a year for 15 years, plus additional milestone payments tied to each building permit issued. Several residents spoke against the deal before the vote, and WVIA reported the agreement does not include the Mid Valley School District, which will host the project's tax and infrastructure impact without being a party to the settlement that was supposed to address it.

Why it matters: Olyphant had been fighting ERCOR Triboro in court; this settlement ends that fight by giving the borough guaranteed cash instead of the conditional-use hearings where residents could have pushed for specific limits (noise, setbacks, water use) project by project.

What to learn — a CBA that trades away your public hearing is trading away more than a vote: A community benefits agreement is only as good as what it actually locks in, and what it gives up to get there. Olyphant's deal secures a payment schedule, but it also waives the conditional-use process — the mechanism that would have let the borough attach project-specific conditions as each building came up for approval. And a CBA negotiated by one government body doesn't automatically cover every body with a stake in the outcome: a school district that isn't a signatory has no claim on the settlement, even though it's the one that will see new students, new infrastructure demands, or new tax base depending on how the deal is actually structured. Before your town trades hearings for cash, ask who else is affected and whether they're in the room. See our model CBA clause library and the community page for Olyphant.

Sources: WVIA, Nov 18; Scranton Times-Tribune, Nov 18

6. Effingham County, Georgia approves OpenAI's site plan — and punts its moratorium decision to January

What happened: Effingham County commissioners voted Nov. 17 to approve the preliminary site plan for OpenAI's $20 billion Project Camellia data center campus — now six buildings on more than 1,300 acres, up from an earlier four-building design — while delaying a separate vote on a proposed moratorium on future data center applications until January, according to the Atlanta Journal-Constitution and WTOC. WRDW reported some residents in the crowd shouted "nay" and "shame" as commissioners voted. The AJC reported the site plan approval is the second of three reviews the project must clear before breaking ground, with a more detailed development plan review still to come and construction targeted for early 2027.

Why it matters: We covered the lawsuits piling up against this project in an earlier piece — by November, Effingham County residents had filed a third suit alleging racketeering. None of that litigation stopped this week's vote. A pending lawsuit is not, by itself, a legal bar to a county continuing to approve permits; only a court order — an injunction — can do that, and none has been issued here.

What to learn — "approved" rarely means the last approval, and a lawsuit doesn't pause anything unless a judge says so: Large projects clear review in stages — rezoning, then a preliminary site plan, then a detailed development plan, then permits — and a story that says a project was "approved" is usually describing one stage, not the whole thing. Ask which stage, and what's still ahead. Separately, if you're counting on a lawsuit to slow a project down while it's litigated, check whether anyone has actually asked for — and been granted — a preliminary injunction; otherwise the county keeps approving things on its normal schedule while the case proceeds in parallel. See the Georgia state page and the Project Camellia dossier.

Sources: Atlanta Journal-Constitution, Nov 17; WTOC, Nov 17; WRDW, Nov 18

7. Hillsboro, Oregon extends its data center moratorium for a third time

What happened: The Hillsboro City Council voted on Nov. 17 to extend its data center land-use moratorium another six months, after nearly a dozen residents spoke at the meeting, according to KPTV. The council first adopted a 120-day moratorium in July; Oregon state law caps a single moratorium at 120 days, which is why Hillsboro's pause has had to come back for a council vote more than once rather than run on a single long clock the way Virginia's 12-month pause or Rochester's one-year ban can.

Why it matters: Hillsboro is one of Oregon's established data center hubs, alongside Prineville and The Dalles — see our hidden water cost of a single prompt for what that kind of concentration actually costs a high-desert or suburban water system. City leaders said they still want more research time before writing permanent rules, which is the same justification Loudoun, San Francisco and several towns on our tracker have used for their own extensions this year.

What to learn — your state's statutory cap on moratorium length tells you how often your town has to come back and vote: Virginia caps a legislative pause at 12 months. Oregon caps one at 120 days. Those are wildly different rhythms for the same tool, and neither is a surprise if you read your own state's enabling statute before the first vote. A town that has to renew every four months gives residents four more chances a year to show up and be heard than one that only has to renew once a year — know which clock your own town is on. See the Oregon state page and the community page for Hillsboro.

Sources: KPTV, Nov 18
