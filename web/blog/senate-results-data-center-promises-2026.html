Nine Senate Seats Flipped. Here Is What the Winners Put in Writing About Data Centers.

Nov 16, 2026 · GridWatch AI
election 2026 Senate Texas Ohio North Carolina South Carolina Maine Michigan ratepayers accountability

All 35 Senate races on the 2026 ballot now have a called winner. Thirteen of those winners left a documented position on data centers during the campaign, and twelve of the thirteen promised some version of the same thing: data centers pay their own way. This post lists who promised what, with the source for each, and what none of it guarantees.

All 35 U.S. Senate races on the Nov. 3 ballot now have a called winner, and our Senate race tracker records each one with the page the result was read from. The Associated Press count stands at 56 Democratic seats to 44 Republican, with nine seats changing party, which AP describes as the most flips since 1958.

If you are fighting a data center in your county, the party count is not the useful part. The useful part is what the person who now represents you said about data centers while asking for the job. That is what the tracker was built to hold, and this is the first time it can be read as a list of promises owed rather than a voter guide.

One caution before the list. Every result here is a news organization's call or projection. None had been certified by a state when we read them. Alaska's certification is targeted for Nov. 25, and Mississippi, where the winner led by about 2,600 votes, certifies Dec. 3, according to AP's rundown of outstanding races. The Justice Department had sued to block certification in Alaska; on Nov. 19 it told the appeals court it is no longer pursuing the case, and the state's elections director has said she intends to certify the result.

The count

Of the 35 winners, 13 have a documented position on data centers in our records. Twelve of those 13 lean toward what we label guardrails: a documented position that data centers should carry their own costs, or that the host community should get a say. One is mixed. One more, Chris Pappas of New Hampshire, is on the record only for the House's near-unanimous vote on the Ratepayer Protection Act, which we show but do not score. The other 21 winners have no record we could find and source, and they are shown that way on the tracker rather than as neutral.

Three candidates in the tracker had a record that leaned the other way, toward building faster without a matching cost or community safeguard on the record. All three were Republicans on a general election ballot, in Alaska, Iowa and North Carolina. All three lost.

That is a small sample, and other things were going on in this election. But it is the complete set, so it is worth stating plainly.

The seats that changed hands

Six of the nine flipped seats went to a candidate with a record on our page.

  • Texas: James Talarico. In July he released a plan to end the state sales-tax break for data centers, require closed-loop water systems, set federal minimum standards and let localities approve or reject projects. His opponent, Ken Paxton, later put out his own plan that also backed repealing the exemption, as FOX 7 Austin reported in September. In Texas, both major candidates ended the race against the tax break.
  • Ohio: Sherrod Brown. He ran ads against Jon Husted's record on developer tax breaks and pledged to make data centers pay the full cost of their electricity.
  • North Carolina: Roy Cooper. His campaign's position was that data centers must pay for all the energy they use with nothing passed to consumers. He, like his opponent, supported letting communities block a project, per Spectrum News. The same report noted that numerous data centers were built while he was governor.
  • South Carolina: Annie Andrews. She ran on a two-year moratorium and local control, WSOC-TV reported. Her opponent was the largest general-election bet by AI-focused super PACs, at about $4.8 million, according to Fortune's tally.
  • Maine: Troy Jackson. He set conditions for data centers: union jobs, fair taxes, environmental protection, community engagement and covering their own power costs, as summarized in Newsweek's survey of Senate candidates.
  • Iowa: Josh Turek. This is the mixed one. He echoed local moratorium sentiment but published no data center platform we could find.

The other three flips, in Alaska (Mary Peltola), Kansas (Adam Hamilton) and Mississippi (Scott Colom), went to candidates with no documented data center position in our records. If you live in one of those states and have a source, send it to us.

The seats that did not change hands

Several returning or newly elected senators in held seats are on the record too:

  • Michigan: Abdul El-Sayed published "terms of engagement" for data center proposals, including protections meant to ensure projects do not raise electric rates.
  • Minnesota: Peggy Flanagan opposes major tax breaks for Big Tech and thinks the use of NDAs for data centers should be banned, according to a Forum News Service rundown of candidates' positions.
  • Virginia: Mark Warner signed on to the Power for the People Act, which would direct states to consider separate utility rate classes for data centers. New Jersey's Cory Booker is also a cosponsor.
  • New Mexico: Ben Ray Luján said data centers should not use drinking or agricultural water and should generate their own power, in a KVIA voter guide.
  • Florida: Ashley Moody, the one Republican winner with a record here, cosponsored the Senate Ratepayer Protection Act.

How much the issue mattered

The NBC News exit poll found 76% of voters opposed a new data center in their own community, and that group voted Democratic 65 to 34. Asked how much AI data centers mattered to their vote for Congress, 23% said crucial and 33% said a major factor.

Read that carefully. It is a rating voters gave when asked about the issue directly. A pre-election NBC poll found only 3% called AI data centers the most important issue facing the country, as Reason noted. Both things are true: most voters do not want one nearby, and for most of them it was not the main reason they voted the way they did.

What a promise is worth

Nothing on this list is an action yet. A campaign plan is a press release. The tracker separates the two on purpose, and the officials scorecard is where votes and signed laws go.

The first test is already on the calendar. On Nov. 9 the outgoing Senate voted 73-25 to take up a permitting bill that would make data center owners pay for the infrastructure built to power them. That was a vote to begin debate, not final passage, and some Democrats want to wait for the new Congress. The new senators are sworn in in January.

For a resident, the practical use of this list is narrow and real. When your senator's office tells you siting is a local matter, you can answer with the sentence they published during the campaign and the link to it.
