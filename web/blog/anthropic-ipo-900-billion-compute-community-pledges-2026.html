Anthropic Went Public With $900 Billion in Compute Commitments. Here Are the Promises It Made to the Towns That Host It.

Nov 20, 2026 · GridWatch AI
Anthropic IPO Texas Navarro County Corsicana Riot Platforms community benefit agreement demand response natural gas tax incentives

Anthropic began trading on Nov. 19. Its prospectus discloses about $900.9 billion in commitments, nearly all for compute and data centers, and repeats a pledge to cover electricity price increases its data centers cause. The company has also published commitments on tax incentives, NDAs and community benefit agreements. The prospectus names no sites. One landlord's filing does, in Navarro County, Texas, and its terms are worth reading against the pledges.

Anthropic, the company behind the Claude AI models, began trading on the Nasdaq on Nov. 19 after raising $81.3 billion, which Bisnow described as the largest stock market debut on record.

The part of the offering that matters to a host community is in the final prospectus filed with the SEC. It discloses committed spending of about $900.9 billion, which the company says is substantially all for compute and infrastructure. About $451.6 billion of that is expected to be spent before the end of 2030. The total does not include a further $150 billion to $175 billion in arrangements with no fixed dollar amount.

That money becomes buildings, substations and power plants in specific counties. The prospectus does not say which ones. It contains no megawatt figures and no U.S. site names. To find out where Anthropic is going, a resident has to read its landlords' filings instead. This post does that for one site and sets it beside what the company has promised.

What Anthropic has promised

Anthropic has made more specific public commitments to host communities than most companies in this business. They are in three places.

In the prospectus. The company states that it has publicly committed to covering the cost of electricity price increases to consumers caused by its data centers, including both the electricity and the infrastructure to deliver it. It tells investors these commitments have raised its operating costs and will continue to. A promise repeated in an SEC filing carries more weight than one in a blog post, because misstating it to investors has consequences.

In its February announcement. In a post titled "Covering electricity price increases", Anthropic said it would pay for the grid upgrades needed to connect its data centers, work to bring new generation online, and cover demand-driven price effects where that generation is not yet built. For capacity it leases rather than owns, the same post says it is exploring further ways to do this. That is softer language.

On its policy page. A page titled "Our approach to responsible data center development" says the company will not accept local tax incentives for future sites, will commit tens of millions of dollars to a community benefit agreement for each data center, will not ask public officials to sign nondisclosure agreements, and will publish a market-by-market method for calculating its effect on power prices. It says the commitments apply to every site Anthropic owns or leases. The page carries no date, and we could not find the published method.

On the day of the listing, co-founder Daniela Amodei told CNBC that the company needs to listen to what host communities want and repeated the electricity pledge, according to CNBC's transcript.

One site on paper: Corsicana, Texas

Riot Platforms, a bitcoin mining company, names Anthropic as a tenant in its quarterly report filed Nov. 4. According to that filing:

  • Anthropic leased land and power infrastructure at Riot's campus in Corsicana, in Navarro County, for 910 megawatts of computing load, delivered in three phases from June 2027 to July 2028.
  • The initial term runs until 15 years after the last phase is delivered, with two five-year extensions.
  • The lease is triple-net: the tenant bears taxes, insurance, site expenses and the cost of electricity.
  • A second lease covers 191 megawatts at Riot's site in Rockdale.

One clause deserves attention from anyone who follows the Texas grid. The filing says Riot may not curtail the tenant's power load, other than as mandatorily ordered by the utility or grid operator, or enroll the premises in voluntary demand response programs without the tenant's consent.

Riot's bitcoin mining, by the same filing, takes part in demand response, which means it can be paid to switch off when the grid is short. Mining is easy to interrupt. AI computing is not. Under this lease, load that used to be available to the grid in a pinch becomes load that stays on unless the grid operator orders otherwise or Anthropic agrees.

That is not a broken promise. Anthropic's February post says it will invest in ways to reduce its draw at peak times, and nothing in the lease stops it from consenting. But the lease puts that decision with the tenant, and residents of a state that has been through grid emergencies are entitled to ask how it will be used.

Questions the documents leave open

None of what follows is an accusation. These are the things we looked for and did not find.

  1. Is there a signed community benefit agreement at Corsicana or Rockdale? The policy page promises tens of millions per data center, and says the company committed millions to repair a water system near one Texas data center, without naming the town. We found no signed agreement at any named site.
  2. Does the tax-incentive pledge cover a leased site with a history? Navarro County commissioners declined a reinvestment zone Riot requested in 2024 after residents objected. We have not confirmed what, if any, abatement applies to the site today. Because the lease makes Anthropic bear the property taxes, the question of whether "no local tax incentives at future sites" covers an existing landlord arrangement is one for the company and the county.
  3. Where is the price method? The pledge to cover price increases depends on how the increase is measured. Until the method is published, nobody outside the company can check the arithmetic.
  4. How much of the power is gas? The prospectus says some planned facilities rely on dedicated on-site natural gas generation, with fuel costs passed through to Anthropic without a cap. The policy page says much of its new power is natural gas. On-site gas plants need air permits, and those have public comment periods.

It is not only Texas

The prospectus warns investors that opposition to data centers has become more organized and that the company's public profile may draw particular attention to projects associated with it. A week of news from Australia shows what that looks like. Reuters reported on Nov. 20 that a developer's filing for a 1.44-gigawatt campus about 250 kilometers west of Brisbane, with Anthropic as anchor tenant, drew nearly 600 public submissions, of which 595 opposed it or asked for delay (via ThePrint).

How to use this if Anthropic is coming to your county

The useful thing about written commitments is that you can hold someone to them. If a developer in your county says the tenant is Anthropic, print the policy page and bring it to the meeting. Then ask, on the record:

  1. Will you sign a community benefit agreement here, for how much, and who enforces it? Our model clauses show what enforceable language looks like.
  2. Is this site receiving any local tax abatement, including one granted to the landlord?
  3. Has any official been asked to sign a nondisclosure agreement, by you or by the developer?
  4. What is your estimate of this site's effect on local electricity prices, and how was it calculated?
  5. Will this load take part in demand response?

A company that has published these commitments should be able to answer all five. Record the answers.
